# Case Study: Global Cocktail Trends Carousel

This case study captures the real production lessons behind the skill.

## Starting point

The initial topic was broad: global cocktail trends. The first content pool included:

1. Agave classics.
2. Lower-ABV serves.
3. Tiny cocktails.
4. Savoury Martinis.
5. Global pantry ingredients.
6. Reworked classics.
7. A stronger sense of place.

The intended audience was bartenders, F&B professionals, and cocktail enthusiasts. The content needed to feel educational, current, personal, and worth saving without presenting the creator as an absolute authority.

## Core user feedback

- “What will the viewer actually learn?”
- The next post must not repeat the previous carousel.
- Brand integration should be subtle.
- The target audience must be explicit.
- The tone should feel like personal observation, not a declaration about the whole industry.
- Visual consistency matters across the entire sequence, not only on individual slides.
- Approved slides should not be rebuilt when only a few slides need correction.

These comments changed the workflow: audience value and editorial angle became approval gates before production.

## Strategic decisions

- Replace deterministic framing such as “these trends will define…” with grounded observation.
- Keep the seven-theme structure because the ideas remained distinct, while materially refining five trend slides: Agave, Savoury Martini, Global Pantry, Classics Rewritten, and Sense of Place.
- Use a context slide to frame the behaviour shift before listing trends.
- End with a practical checklist rather than a generic promotional CTA.
- Provide three caption voices: personal observation, personal learning, and humble discussion.

## Visual system

- 4:5 Instagram portrait.
- 1080 × 1350 px final exports.
- Off-white paper background.
- Black serif headlines.
- Large persimmon numbering.
- Wasabi accents.
- Bright editorial studio lighting.
- Controlled negative space.
- Full glasses and visible garnishes.

## Review and corrections

The review loop focused on:

- Repeated ideas.
- Weak educational value.
- Overconfident language.
- Visual imbalance.
- Inconsistent number colour and scale.
- Title placement.
- Text near edges.
- Cropped glasses and garnishes.
- Lighting and background inconsistency.
- Caption voice.

The strongest revision rule was scope control: change only the slides named in the feedback and preserve approved work.

## Technical delivery

The final package contained:

- Ten ordered carousel images.
- Three caption options.
- Upload instructions.
- A Canva master link.
- Zero-padded filenames.
- Standardised 1080 × 1350 px exports.

The source images initially measured 1122 × 1402 px and were normalised for Instagram. A later archive check found that one extracted slide could not be decoded even though the ZIP itself passed integrity testing. This proved that archive integrity, file size, and image dimensions are not enough: every extracted image must be opened or decoded before delivery.

## What succeeded

- The content moved from a generic trend list to a clear editorial observation.
- The audience and learning value became easier to explain.
- The seven ideas formed a coherent sequence.
- The visual system became recognisable across all slides.
- The final package was ordered and practical to upload.
- Caption choices protected the creator’s personal and humble voice.
- The project produced reusable rules for future F&B carousels.

## What failed or slowed the process

- Starting design before fully locking the learning outcome.
- Repeating territory already covered by an earlier carousel.
- Treating every trend as equally strong.
- Iterating with many temporary filenames and audit screenshots.
- Mixing flattened delivery images with the idea of an editable automation template.
- Assuming a file was healthy because it existed and had the expected size.
- Assuming a connected service automatically had write permission.

## Reusable lessons

1. Decide what the viewer learns before deciding how the slide looks.
2. Audit recent posts before choosing an angle.
3. Use research to support a point of view, not replace one.
4. Keep one lesson per slide.
5. Lock reusable visual tokens before production.
6. Treat feedback as scoped change requests.
7. Separate editable masters, drafts, audits, and final exports.
8. Verify every final file by decoding it.
9. Build Canva automation from editable layers, not flattened PNGs.
10. Use post metrics to inform the next angle rather than retroactively changing the goal.
